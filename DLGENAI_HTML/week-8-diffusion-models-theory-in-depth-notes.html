Diffusion models: learn to denoise

From Gaussian corruption to a learned reverse process: equations, schedule numericals, guided sampling, and a complete PyTorch DDPM lab.

DDPM forward processNoise predictionReverse samplingLatent diffusion

How to use these notes

Diffusion is easiest to understand as a deliberately destructive forward process followed by a learned reverse process. The forward direction adds known Gaussian noise; the neural network learns to remove it. For exam numericals, keep α, ᾱ, β, and the predicted noise separate, write the square-root coefficients first, and substitute only at the end.

Exam-pattern note: Direct Quiz 1 coverage of diffusion is limited. The supplied exam notes flag DDPM as a lower-priority direct quiz topic but include it in the formula bank. Accordingly, this guide teaches the full process and uses exam-style coefficient substitution, schedule interpretation, tensor-shape tracing, and “which quantity is predicted?” distractors rather than treating these examples as actual past questions.

1. Why diffusion can generate images

1.1 The forward Markov chain

Take a clean data example x₀. Choose a variance schedule β₁,…,βT, with small positive βt<1. Define αt=1−βt. The fixed forward transition is

q(xt|xt−1)=N(√αtxt−1, βtI)

Each transition slightly shrinks signal and adds independent Gaussian noise. Repeating this T times gradually removes recognizable structure. The forward transitions are chosen by us; they are not the learned generator.

1.2 Closed-form sample at any timestep

Let ᾱt=∏s=1tαs. Because Gaussians compose, one can sample xₜ from x₀ directly:

q(xt|x₀)=N(√ᾱtx₀,(1−ᾱt)I)
xt=√ᾱtx₀ + √(1−ᾱt)ε, ε~N(0,I)

This formula is central: during training, sample random t and ε; construct xₜ in one step instead of simulating every earlier transition. ᾱ measures retained signal: near 1, xₜ is close to x₀; near 0, xₜ is mostly noise.

1.3 Schedule intuition and signal-to-noise ratio

A β schedule controls how quickly information is destroyed. Linear schedules are simple; cosine-style schedules distribute signal degradation differently. Define signal-to-noise ratio SNR(t)=ᾱt/(1−ᾱt). At early t, SNR is large; late t, it is small. A schedule must make xT close enough to the chosen prior (typically standard Gaussian) for reverse generation to start from that prior without a large train/test mismatch.

Notation trap: βt is the variance added at step t; αt=1−βt is one-step signal retention; ᾱt is the cumulative product of all α values through t. Do not use α and ᾱ interchangeably.

2. Train a denoiser to predict noise

2.1 Noise-prediction objective

Given a clean x₀, choose t uniformly, sample ε~N(0,I), and create xₜ from the closed form. A neural network receives both xₜ and t and predicts the injected noise:

Lsimple=Ex₀,t,ε[||ε−εθ(xt,t)||²]

The timestep matters: a model must know whether its input is lightly or heavily corrupted. A U-Net predicts a noise tensor with the same spatial shape/channels as ε. The widely used simple objective is related to optimizing a variational bound; it is not literally “compare predicted clean image to noise.”

2.2 Why a U-Net?

Diffusion denoisers need both large context and fine spatial detail. U-Net downsampling blocks expand receptive field; skip connections bring fine features back during upsampling. Timestep embeddings are injected into blocks so the network can adapt to noise level. For text/class conditional models, embeddings can modulate features or enter through cross-attention.

2.3 Small code: forward corruption

# abar is the cumulative alpha product, indexed from 0 for t=1
noise = torch.randn_like(x0)
t = torch.randint(0, T, (x0.size(0),), device=x0.device)
a_bar_t = abar[t].view(-1, 1, 1, 1)
xt = a_bar_t.sqrt() * x0 + (1.0 - a_bar_t).sqrt() * noise
predicted_noise = denoiser(xt, t)
loss = F.mse_loss(predicted_noise, noise)

The same per-example coefficient is broadcast over channel/height/width. Indexing errors often come from mixing mathematical timesteps 1…T with Python indices 0…T−1.

3. Worked diffusion numericals

Numerical A: mix signal and noise

Suppose ᾱt=0.64, x₀=2, ε=−1. Then √ᾱ=.8 and √(1−ᾱ)=.6, so xₜ=.8(2)+.6(−1)=1.6−.6=1.0. The clean signal coefficient is .8 and the noise coefficient is .6.

Numerical B: alternate values

Suppose ᾱ=.25, x₀=4, ε=2. Coefficients are √.25=.5 and √.75≈.8660. Thus xₜ=.5(4)+.8660(2)=2+1.732=3.732. Notice the noise contributes positively because this sampled ε was positive.

Numerical C: cumulative schedule

Let β₁=.1 and β₂=.2. Then α₁=.9, α₂=.8; ᾱ₂=.9×.8=.72. For x₀=1, ε=.5 at t=2, x₂=√.72×1+√.28×.5≈.8485+.2646=1.1131. Do not add β values to get ᾱ.

Numerical D: one reverse mean and variance

Use α=.9, β=.1, ᾱt=.72, ᾱt−1=.8, xₜ=1.2, predicted εθ=.5. The DDPM noise-parameterized reverse mean is

μθ(xt,t)=1/√αt[xt−βt/√(1−ᾱt) · εθ]

Substitute: 1/√.9 × [1.2−.1/√.28×.5] ≈1.0541×(1.2−.0945)=1.1653. The posterior variance is β̃t=βt(1−ᾱt−1)/(1−ᾱt)=.1×.2/.28=.07143; standard deviation≈.2673. If z=.25, xt−1=μ+√β̃ z≈1.1653+.2673(.25)=1.2321. At final t=1, omit the extra Gaussian noise term to return the clean estimate.

Check: In the forward formula, coefficient of x₀ is √ᾱ and coefficient of ε is √(1−ᾱ). In the reverse formula, the model predicts εθ, then a schedule-dependent formula computes the mean. It does not directly output xt−1.

4. Reverse sampling and conditioning

4.1 DDPM reverse step

At generation time, begin with xT~N(0,I). For t=T,…,1, predict εθ(xₜ,t), compute the reverse mean, optionally add Gaussian noise, and step to xt−1. Repeating T steps yields an image. In the simplified DDPM parameterization the variance may be fixed to β̃t or another schedule choice; the paper and implementation specify it. At t=1, do not inject another noise draw.

xt−1=μθ(xt,t)+√β̃tz, z~N(0,I), t>1

Sampling cost is proportional to the number of denoising steps because each step invokes the network. Fewer-step samplers trade time against quality and are design-dependent.

4.2 DDPM vs DDIM

DDPM is the classic stochastic reverse chain with a particular learned or fixed reverse variance. DDIM constructs a family of non-Markovian sampling trajectories sharing the training objective; a common setting is deterministic for a fixed initial noise. DDIM can skip steps. Neither means “diffusion takes one forward pass”—generation still follows a sequence of denoising updates.

4.3 Classifier-free guidance

Train the conditional denoiser sometimes without its condition so it learns both εuncond and εcond. At inference:

εguided=εuncond+w(εcond−εuncond)

w=0 gives the unconditional prediction; w=1 gives the conditional prediction; w>1 extrapolates toward stronger condition agreement. Larger guidance can reduce diversity or create artifacts. Classifier guidance instead uses gradients of a separate classifier with respect to xₜ.

4.4 Latent diffusion

Pixel-space diffusion operates on high-resolution RGB tensors and is expensive. Latent diffusion first encodes an image with an autoencoder to a smaller spatial latent, diffuses and denoises there, and decodes the final latent. Text/image conditions can interact through cross-attention. Compression lowers compute but the autoencoder bottleneck constrains detail. Stable-diffusion-style systems combine an image autoencoder, diffusion U-Net, text encoder, and scheduler; “Stable Diffusion is only a GAN” is false.

5. Common concept and code traps

ClaimCorrect interpretation
“The model predicts x₀ in the basic objective.”The common simple DDPM objective trains εθ to predict sampled noise ε. Other parameterizations (x₀ or v prediction) also exist, but identify which the question states.
“The network adds noise during generation.”The fixed forward process adds noise for training; reverse generation starts noisy and the learned model denoises. A sampler may add stochastic noise in reverse steps.
“ᾱ is the current β.”ᾱₜ is a cumulative product of αₛ values.
“t is irrelevant after xₜ is formed.”The same xₜ value distribution can arise at different noise levels; t tells the network the corruption level.
“At the last denoising step, always add noise.”The terminal x₀ estimate commonly omits the stochastic term.
“Higher classifier-free guidance always improves quality.”It strengthens conditioning but can reduce diversity and add artifacts.
“Noise prediction target shape is [B].”ε has the full image shape [B,C,H,W], and εθ must match it.

6. Exam-pattern practice with worked answers

Q1. In the common DDPM training objective, what does εθ(xₜ,t) try to predict?

The specific Gaussian noise ε that was sampled to create xₜ from x₀. The target has the same shape as x₀.

Q2. If ᾱ=.64, x₀=2, ε=−1, compute xₜ.

√.64=.8; √.36=.6; xₜ=.8×2+.6×(−1)=1.0.

Q3. Given β₁=.1 and β₂=.2, what is ᾱ₂?

α₁=.9, α₂=.8, hence ᾱ₂=.9×.8=.72.

Q4. What does timestep conditioning provide?

It informs the denoiser of the noise level/corruption stage, which determines how strongly image signal is retained and how much noise is present.

Q5. Under classifier-free guidance, what happens at w=0 and w=1?

w=0 yields ε_uncond; w=1 algebraically yields ε_cond. Values above 1 extrapolate toward the conditional direction.

Q6. A code question shows noise [B,3,32,32] and prediction [B,1,32,32]. Is ordinary elementwise MSE valid?

No: shapes do not match and broadcasting could produce a misleading tensor. The denoiser output must predict all 3 noise channels at every spatial location: [B,3,32,32].

7. Complete end-of-week program: tiny DDPM on MNIST

This compact example trains a small time-conditioned convolutional noise predictor on 28×28 MNIST images. It shows schedule construction, one-step corruption, noise-prediction training, and the full reverse sampler. This is a teaching implementation, not a high-quality production U-Net or a benchmark reproduction.

"""Week 8 lab: a small DDPM noise-prediction model on MNIST."""
import math
import torch
from torch import nn
from torch.nn import functional as F
from torch.utils.data import DataLoader
from torchvision import datasets, transforms

SEED, T = 17, 100
torch.manual_seed(SEED)
device = torch.device("cuda" if torch.cuda.is_available() else "cpu")

# MNIST is normalized to [-1,1], matching the example's data convention.
dataset = datasets.MNIST(
    root="data", train=True, download=True,
    transform=transforms.Compose([
        transforms.ToTensor(),
        transforms.Lambda(lambda x: 2.0 * x - 1.0)
    ])
)
loader = DataLoader(dataset, batch_size=128, shuffle=True,
                    drop_last=True, num_workers=0)

# ------------------------- Schedule --------------------------
betas = torch.linspace(1e-4, 0.02, T, device=device)
alpha = 1.0 - betas
abar = torch.cumprod(alpha, dim=0)
abar_prev = torch.cat([torch.ones(1, device=device), abar[:-1]])
posterior_var = betas * (1.0 - abar_prev) / (1.0 - abar)

# ------------------- Time-conditioned CNN -------------------
def timestep_embedding(t, dim=32):
    """Sinusoidal embedding; t is integer shape [B]."""
    half = dim // 2
    scale = math.log(10000.0) / max(half - 1, 1)
    freq = torch.exp(torch.arange(half, device=t.device) * -scale)
    phase = t.float().unsqueeze(1) * freq.unsqueeze(0)
    emb = torch.cat([torch.sin(phase), torch.cos(phase)], dim=1)
    if dim % 2:
        emb = F.pad(emb, (0, 1))
    return emb

class TinyDenoiser(nn.Module):
    def __init__(self, time_dim=32, channels=64):
        super().__init__()
        self.time_mlp = nn.Sequential(
            nn.Linear(time_dim, channels), nn.SiLU(),
            nn.Linear(channels, channels)
        )
        self.conv1 = nn.Conv2d(1, channels, 3, padding=1)
        self.norm1 = nn.GroupNorm(8, channels)
        self.conv2 = nn.Conv2d(channels, channels, 3, padding=1)
        self.norm2 = nn.GroupNorm(8, channels)
        self.out = nn.Conv2d(channels, 1, 3, padding=1)

    def forward(self, x, t):
        temb = self.time_mlp(timestep_embedding(t))[:, :, None, None]
        h = F.silu(self.norm1(self.conv1(x)))
        h = h + temb                       # broadcast over H,W
        residual = h
        h = F.silu(self.norm2(self.conv2(h)))
        h = h + residual                   # simple residual block
        return self.out(F.silu(h))         # predicted ε, [B,1,28,28]

model = TinyDenoiser().to(device)
optimizer = torch.optim.Adam(model.parameters(), lr=2e-4)

# ------------------------- Training --------------------------
for epoch in range(3):
    model.train()
    total_loss = 0.0
    for x0, _ in loader:
        x0 = x0.to(device)                 # [B,1,28,28]
        b = x0.size(0)
        t = torch.randint(0, T, (b,), device=device)
        eps = torch.randn_like(x0)
        a_bar = abar[t].view(b, 1, 1, 1)
        xt = a_bar.sqrt() * x0 + (1.0-a_bar).sqrt() * eps

        optimizer.zero_grad(set_to_none=True)
        eps_hat = model(xt, t)
        loss = F.mse_loss(eps_hat, eps)    # target = sampled noise
        loss.backward()
        nn.utils.clip_grad_norm_(model.parameters(), max_norm=1.0)
        optimizer.step()
        total_loss += loss.item()
    print(f"epoch {epoch+1}: mean noise-prediction MSE="
          f"{total_loss/len(loader):.4f}")

# --------------------- Reverse sampling ----------------------
@torch.no_grad()
def sample_ddpm(n=16):
    model.eval()
    x = torch.randn(n, 1, 28, 28, device=device)  # x_T ~ N(0,I)
    for ti in reversed(range(T)):
        t = torch.full((n,), ti, device=device, dtype=torch.long)
        eps_hat = model(x, t)
        a = alpha[ti]
        b = betas[ti]
        a_bar = abar[ti]
        # Posterior mean for the epsilon parameterization.
        mean = (x - (b / torch.sqrt(1.0-a_bar)) * eps_hat) / torch.sqrt(a)
        if ti > 0:
            noise = torch.randn_like(x)
            x = mean + torch.sqrt(posterior_var[ti]) * noise
        else:
            x = mean                         # no noise after final step
    return x.clamp(-1.0, 1.0)

samples = sample_ddpm(16)
print("sample batch:", samples.shape,
      "range:", samples.min().item(), samples.max().item())
# To display/save with torchvision.utils.save_image, map [-1,1] back to [0,1].
images_for_display = (samples + 1.0) / 2.0

Line-by-line reasoning

  1. Schedule: abar[t] stores the product through zero-based index t. abar_prev[0]=1 represents the clean boundary.
  2. Time embedding: an integer step becomes a fixed sinusoidal vector, then an MLP maps it to channel width. The feature map receives that vector by broadcasting across height/width.
  3. Corruption: one random t is chosen per image; a separate ε tensor is sampled. x_t is made in one closed-form step.
  4. Objective: network output and target noise have identical [B,1,28,28] shape. MSE teaches the network to estimate the injected noise.
  5. Reverse mean: learned ε̂ is inserted into the schedule-derived DDPM mean.
  6. Posterior variance: posterior_var[t] adds reverse randomness for t>0; t=0 returns the final mean.
  7. Range: training images are [-1,1], while the initial noise is unbounded Gaussian. Clamping is a display convenience and can slightly alter the mathematical sampler.
  8. Compute: each sample invokes the denoiser T times. Increasing T raises sampling cost; a short schedule in this teaching example may yield poor images.
Model limitation: this small same-resolution CNN is not a full U-Net, uses no class/text conditioning, and trains for only three epochs. Production diffusion systems require larger datasets, stronger denoisers, tuned schedules, robust evaluation, and more compute.

8. Week 8 formula card and final checklist

Schedule

αₜ=1−βₜ; ᾱₜ=∏s≤tαₛ.

Direct forward sample

xₜ=√ᾱₜx₀+√(1−ᾱₜ)ε.

Training target

εθ(xₜ,t) predicts ε; minimize mean squared error.

Reverse mean

μθ=1/√αₜ[xₜ−βₜ/√(1−ᾱₜ) εθ(xₜ,t)].
